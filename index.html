01 / About

Where the work sits

Born and raised in Naharlagun, Arunachal Pradesh, the northeasternmost part of India, where the sun rises first, I didn't have extensive exposure to computer science or technology in general. No coding bootcamps, no high-school startup garages. What I did have was curiosity: about the mechanics of an equation, about why people make the choices they do, and about what happens when you mix the right spices in the wrong order.

Having learnt Python for the first time in high school, my prior interest in mathematics was accompanied by tinges of coding, which led to a little exploration, and so I chose CSE as my major, it appeared to me to be one of the most direct applications of mathematics.

I like working around problems concerning humans and solving them using AI. AI for humans, or humans for AI? Both ways, actually. So I'm interested in human-centred intelligent systems, and the project that aligns most closely with that is focused on documenting and reviving the traditional handloom motifs of the tribes of Arunachal Pradesh. It is taking a lot of time, since the dataset is still not extensive enough.

One of my areas of interest is representation learning. Of late I've also been working on applications of optimal transport theory, specifically in transformer fusion across heterogeneous depths. The work is still in progress since its onset at CCU, Taiwan, where I interned under the Taiwan Education Exchange Program (TEEP) in the summer of 2026, and I hope to publish it soon.

Apart from all this, I'm a connoisseur of cuisines around the world and have loved cooking since I was eight. I also write blogs, which you can read on Medium. Although I loved reading through my teenage years, that habit has transcended more into writing now : mostly random thoughts mixed with nostalgic fragments that occur to me at times.

Most recent

Heterogeneous transformer fusion along statistical manifolds: a Bures–Wasserstein approach

Dept. of Computer Science and Information Engineering. The work develops a multi-scale fusion pipeline and, more importantly, examines which component actually drives its performance. Through an analytical study of Wasserstein barycentric interpolation under the Bures metric, I show that the fused weights depend on distributional statistics that are invariant to row permutations, meaning permutation alignment is not the mechanism responsible for the observed gains.

Experiments across multiple alignment settings support this: hard permutation alignment is statistically indistinguishable from no alignment, while replacing Bures interpolation with Euclidean interpolation substantially degrades zero-shot fusion performance. The benefit of optimal-transport-based fusion, in this setting, comes from distributional matching of weight statistics rather than resolving permutation symmetry.

Optimal Transport Fused Gromov–Wasserstein Sinkhorn Vision Transformers

Drone surveillance & trajectory prediction

Under Dr Dinesh Singh, I worked on two problems at the intersection of optimisation and machine learning. First: scheduling drone patrols to minimise flight time under patrol-interval constraints — ILP, Greedy, Simulated Annealing and a novel multi-stage method, tested on real campus maps (Ritsumeikan University, IISER Bhopal) for high coverage at reduced runtime. Second: the Trajectron model for multi-agent trajectory prediction using CVAEs and dynamic spatiotemporal graphs, evaluated on ETH/UCY with competitive ADE/FDE/NLL and outperforming baselines such as SGAN.

Heterogeneous transformer fusion along statistical manifolds: a Bures–Wasserstein approachCCU TaiwanOngoing

Fusing heterogeneous vision transformers by formulating model fusion as a computational graph alignment problem. Fused Gromov–Wasserstein transport handles the macro structure, Wasserstein barycentric synthesis resolves the depth mismatch without heuristic identity padding, and Sinkhorn alignment handles parameters.

Optimal Transport · FGW · Sinkhorn
